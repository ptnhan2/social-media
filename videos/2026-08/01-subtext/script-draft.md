You just spent three hours writing with AI. The grammar is perfect. The structure is sound. And something is... off. Your characters say exactly what they mean. Nobody talks around anything. It reads like a textbook wearing a novel's clothes. You think the problem is your prompt. It's not. The problem is how AI was trained, and once you see it, you can't unsee it. Remember that. We'll come back to it.

So what is subtext? It's when a character says A but means B. The reader figures it out themselves. Hemingway wrote an entire story about abortion without ever using the word "abortion." The reader has to infer. That's what makes fiction feel alive. It trusts the reader enough to let them figure it out.

Now imagine AI is an employee who's been trained since childhood that clarity is good and ambiguity is bad. Always explain. Always be helpful. Always be explicit. You tell this employee: "Don't explain, just imply." They look at you like you're asking them to betray everything they were taught.

And that's exactly what's happening. AI is trained through RLHF. Basically, it's how AI gets raised, like raising a child: reward when good, punish when bad. AI gets rewarded for being helpful, clear, and explicit. The model learns: always explain. When it writes "she felt sadness," it's doing exactly what it was trained to do.

It used to take writers five or ten years to master subtext. Now you're asking AI to do it in one prompt. It's like telling someone who's always been honest to stop being honest. You're not changing a style. You're fighting their nature.

Here's where it gets interesting. NailedIt.ai, a site that tests AI side-by-side like a blind taste test for wine, ran an experiment. They asked both Claude and ChatGPT to write a scene where a character is grieving, without using the words "sad," "cry," "tears," or "heartbreak." The goal: can AI show grief through actions instead of telling it through emotions?

Claude's response: the character waters plants that are already dead, orders coffee by mistake (muscle memory of ordering for two), holds two cups, one for someone who's no longer there. You read this and you feel the grief. Nobody had to say "she was sad." Claude, in this short scene, actually pulled off subtext.

ChatGPT's response: "Just like the stain on the plate, the stain on his heart would never wash away." Sounds literary. But it's explaining the grief through a metaphor instead of making you feel it. You read it and think "nice metaphor." But you don't feel anything.

So Claude succeeded? In one scene, yes. But here's the real problem: you don't write a novel in one scene. You write 80,000 words. And according to the ACL, the scientific body for AI language research (like the FDA but for AI), a 2026 paper called "Narrative Flattening" proves that the longer AI writes, the more it loses the ability to do subtext. RLHF suppresses ambiguity. AI can override its training in short bursts. But the longer it writes, the more training bias pulls it back to "helpful, clear, explicit." Subtext fades. "She felt sadness" creeps back in.

And it's not just experts who notice. On Reddit's r/WritingWithAI, a community of 158,000 writers who use AI, one post got 214 upvotes and 77 comments. Writer after writer said the same thing: "It defaults to naming emotions rather than evoking them through concrete detail. 'She felt sadness' vs making the reader feel it through sensory specifics. This required the most manual rewriting."

So, are you stuck with soulless AI prose forever? No. But the fix isn't "better prompts." The fix is understanding that AI was trained to do the opposite of fiction, then writing prompts that specifically counteract that training.

Three things. But don't think of these as tips. These are countermeasures. Each one directly fights something RLHF taught AI to do.

One: Never let AI name emotions. "Describe what the character DOES, not what they FEEL." You're directly fighting the training that says "be explicit about emotions." AI will not want to do this. You have to force it.

Two: Give AI the subtext separately. "The character is angry about X but will never mention X directly." AI was trained to disclose, not to keep secrets. You're telling it to do something it was taught not to do.

Three: Constrain the dialogue. "Characters must talk about Y while the real tension is about X." You're creating distance between what's said and what's meant. That distance is subtext. AI naturally closes that distance. You have to recreate it.

At the start of this video, I said: once you understand the reason, you'll never look at AI fiction the same way. Here's the reason:

AI isn't broken. It's optimized to be helpful, clear, and explicit. But fiction doesn't need helpful. Fiction needs trust — trust that the reader is smart enough to figure it out on their own. AI will always want to explain. Your job is to let it imply.

Your prompts don't fix AI. Your prompts bridge the gap between two completely different purposes.

And that's something five years of writing school never taught you, because before AI, nobody needed to learn how to fight training.
